Literature Survey

A comprehensive analysis of e-commerce packaging waste, circular economy models, and the environmental superiority of reuse over recycling.

1. The E-Commerce Packaging Crisis

The rapid expansion of the e-commerce and quick-commerce sectors in India has resulted in a staggering increase in packaging waste. As of 2025, paper and paperboard account for approximately 50.83% of the Indian e-commerce packaging market. Corrugated cartons alone hold a 45.73% market share due to their structural integrity and suitability for automated fulfillment centers.

According to World Bank reports, packaging materials like paper and cardboard account for nearly 10% of the net waste generated in South Asia. In India, a significant challenge remains: low rates of source segregation mean that vast quantities of structurally sound cardboard end up in landfills or informal dumps rather than being systematically recovered.

2. The Environmental Footprint: Recycling vs. Reuse

While recycling is heavily promoted as a sustainable solution, recent environmental studies highlight the massive energy and water requirements of the cardboard recycling process. Recycling requires transporting, sorting, breaking down the material into a slurry, and pulping it to create new boxes.

The "Embodied Energy" Principle

Reusing a cardboard box leverages its "embodied energy"—the energy already expended to manufacture it. By extending a box's life in its original form, the entire carbon and water footprint of manufacturing or recycling is bypassed. Independent research demonstrates that reusing a box is unequivocally superior to recycling it.

3. Local Supply Chain Economics

On the economic front, India's unorganized retail sector, comprising over 12 million kirana (grocery) and local vendor stores, faces continuous margin pressures. Field surveys and economic analyses indicate that local shopkeepers spend a significant portion of their operational budget on packaging materials.

  • High Procurement Costs: Vendors typically pay between ₹20 and ₹35 for a new corrugated box.
  • Scrap Value Discrepancy: Conversely, consumers or local scrap dealers (kabadiwalas) sell used boxes to aggregators for mere pennies (often ~₹3 to ₹5 per kg) destined for destruction/pulping.
  • The Missing Link: There is a documented lack of formal consumer-to-business (C2B) or community-to-business networks that connect the surplus of intact boxes in residential areas directly to the deficit in local commercial areas.

4. Circular Economy Frameworks and EPR

India is gradually tightening its Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) regulations. While historically focused on plastics, newer frameworks are addressing paper packaging. The principles of a Circular Economy strongly advocate for the "Waste Hierarchy," which dictates that Reuse sits above Recycling.

A standard corrugated box can undergo 5 to 7 reuse cycles before its fibers degrade to a point where recycling is necessary. However, most boxes currently see only a single use. The literature suggests that localized, community-driven closed-loop systems—which minimize transportation distances and maximize material lifespan—are the most effective method for achieving true circularity in urban environments.

5. Conclusion and Gap Identification

The literature highlights a clear gap: while macro-level recycling infrastructure exists (albeit flawed), micro-level reuse infrastructure is virtually nonexistent in India. Consumers face "eco-guilt" and space constraints, discarding boxes prematurely, while local vendors suffer from high packaging costs.

ReBox directly addresses this gap documented in the research by establishing a hyper-local reuse network. By intercepting boxes before they reach the energy-intensive recycling stage or the landfill, ReBox aligns with the highest tiers of environmental sustainability practices identified in current academic and industry literature.
